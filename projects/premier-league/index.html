Goals Scored in the Premier League

Is there a best of the best? An NBD model of every goal scored in the 2023–24 Premier League season.

Course
MKTG/STAT 4760, Wharton
Team
Solo project
Data
Stathead FBref, 2023–24 season, 570 players
Method
NBD with varying time, fit by MLE
The question

Is there a best of the best?

The top-level in sports such as the NBA, NFL, and Premier League pit the best players against each other. There's considerable money to scout and secure these players, as well as trades to get the best on one's team.

Are the best different from each other? What separates the best from the best? These are common questions for any sports fan, but it is also a key business problem. Do the Lakers trade for Doncic and give up Anthony Davis in return? Is Haaland truly better than other scorers, or is he like the rest of the Premier League?

These questions are of heterogeneity, the differences between people. To look specifically at soccer, goals are key count data to investigate this.

The data

570 players, one season

Count data is frequently modeled using the Poisson distribution, but there are some risks with that. The Negative Binomial Distribution accounts for hyper dispersion of data, a key concern in many real-life data sets.

After modeling a few datasets, this soccer dataset proved to be the most interesting. Previous datasets used were accidents in explosive-making factories; NBA 2-point field goals, blocks, and steals; and other soccer data sets. These were either not fit well by NBD models or proved to be unwieldy (Giannis and Jokic score a lot of 2s).

The data comes from Stathead FBref: Premier League, 2023–24 season, every player with at least one minute played. In terms of understanding the story, here are the building blocks.

  • Individual = one soccer player (i.e., Haaland)
  • Observed individual behavior = goals per individual (i.e., 27)
  • Lambda = propensity for a player to score a certain amount of goals
    • Haaland spins his Poisson wheel and scores 27 goals this season. Next season, he'll spin it again and may score 36 goals.
  • r = measure of heterogeneity, key to the business problem
    • High r (r > 1) indicates homogeneity (everyone is the same)
    • Low r (r < 1) indicates heterogeneity (people are different)
  • Alpha = shape parameter, no helpful insights
  • t = time, minutes played

In the specific data, Haaland is at the top with an astonishing 27 goals given 2,552 minutes of game time, a significant 5 goals above the next best with a similar amount of game time. There are some efficient scorers, with Chris Wood scoring 14 goals in only 1,812 minutes of game time. This puts Haaland's production in vaulted territory. This seems to indicate at least Haaland is different from the riff raff, but what about a more general statement?

The models

Four NBD models

A plain NBD model is the baseline to understand the fit to the data. From there, a spike is added. Truncating and shifting are not very relevant to this dataset. The next area of interest is different subgroups, focusing on Forwards and Midfielders.

MLE is the main form of parameter estimation since it captures the data better than Method of Moments or Means and Zeroes. These models assume stationarity and independence.

The key insight is drawn from the r parameter. Across the NBD models on the full dataset, a low r (less than 1) was estimated. A low r indicates high heterogeneity, meaning the soccer players are different from one another.

However, specific analysis of the Forwards and Midfielders subgroup revealed a different insight: a higher r (greater than 1), indicating homogeneity. This increase in homogeneity is to be expected (strikers are more similar to other strikers than defenders), but now the conclusion for strikers is different. Now the results indicate that the FWs and MFs are homogeneous, meaning they're similar to each other.

Pretty good years for a striker may be a lucky spin of the Poisson wheel.

Limitations and next steps

Where this goes next

It's important to note that this is specific to the Premier League in the 2023–24 season. This connects back to the fundamental business problem: is there a best of the best? The analysis for the selected situation indicates a potential no among forwards and midfielders.

To begin with, this analysis should be repeated across seasons and in different leagues, presenting a larger outlook on soccer in general. It would also be interesting to try to predict future seasons' goal distributions using this model. Next, introducing covariates (age, starts, subs/unsubs, etc.) could potentially improve the model or glean useful insights. Other subgroups could produce interesting results as well, such as team or nation.

From there, it would be interesting to compare this with other sports. Is there similar heterogeneity in the NBA, NFL, or so on? In class, it was mentioned that field goal kickers may be homogeneous at the top (similar to what is seen here). What sports have high heterogeneity at the top, which ones don't, and why?

Haaland may still be better than everyone else, but on the whole, the top forwards and midfielders may be more similar to each other than different.
