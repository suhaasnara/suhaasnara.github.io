Hurricane Katrina's Impact on Employment

What was the causal impact of Hurricane Katrina on county level economic outcomes in affected regions?

Course
BEPP 2800, Wharton
Team
Team of six
When
December 2025
Method
Difference-in-differences with county and time fixed effects
The question

A natural disaster as an economic shock

Hurricane Katrina was one of the most severe natural disasters in U.S. history, causing extensive physical destruction, population displacement, and disruption to local labor markets across the Gulf Coast.

The storm's path and intensity created sharp differences in exposure across counties that were largely unrelated to pre-existing economic conditions, generating plausibly exogenous variation in economic outcomes. County-level employment data allows us to observe localized labor market responses to the hurricane and to compare affected and unaffected counties over time.

Natural experiment

Hurricane Katrina (August 2005) as a natural disaster. A sudden shock, geographically concentrated, with no manipulation of treatment.

Identification strategy

Compare affected and unaffected counties before and after the hurricane. Use DiD to account for background effects and estimate an average treatment effect.

Key assumption

Parallel trends: in the absence of Hurricane Katrina, employment trends would be similar across the affected and unaffected counties.

The data

64 Louisiana counties, 20 quarters

County-level quarterly employment and wage data for Louisiana from Kaggle, combined with hurricane exposure indicators for Hurricane Katrina. Each observation is a county in a quarter: a balanced sample of 64 counties over 20 quarters (2003 to 2007), or 1,280 observations. The outcome is quarterly employment, and 20 of the 64 counties are flagged as affected.

While levels differ, the parallel trends assumption is supported by the roughly similar pre-2005 trends. The main figure shows the distinct decline in mean quarterly employment by affected counties. Interestingly, there seems to be a recovery in employment as time progresses.

The model regresses quarterly employment on the treatment interaction (affected county × after 2005 Q3), with county and time fixed effects and standard errors clustered at the county level. County fixed effects control for time-invariant differences in employment level, time fixed effects control for state-wide shocks across counties, and clustered standard errors account for correlation within counties.

The regression estimated an average decrease of approximately 12,800 jobs in quarterly employment for counties affected by Hurricane Katrina. However, this point estimate isn't statistically significant, with the standard error higher than the estimated treatment effect (p-value = 0.368). This indicates that counties likely experienced heterogeneous levels of change in employment.

Why it isn't significant

Not every affected county lost jobs

Each dot is one county: its change in average quarterly employment from before Katrina to after. Built from the team's dataset to show the heterogeneity behind the result.

Visually, there is a noticeable decline. However, it's not statistically significant when we cluster the standard errors at the county level. This may be due to heterogeneous effects, few clusters, and a limited post-period. The findings suggest there isn't a cleanly uniform impact on employment across affected counties.

Limitations

What could bias the result

Significant risk

Spillovers

Spillovers to neighboring counties are a major limitation of this analysis, limiting its validity. Unaffected counties may receive displaced workers or aid in recovery efforts.

Moderate risk

Limited post-period, measurement, and timing

A longer post-period would increase power and the confidence we'd have in any findings. Any issues in reporting or measurement would affect results.

Mild risk

Heterogeneous employment effects

Katrina's impact likely varied across counties. Employment effects may also differ in the short term versus the long term.

Related study

Belasen and Polachek (2007)

Uses a difference-in-differences framework on major hurricanes in Florida. Hurricanes are associated with declines in employment in affected counties, while average earnings increase, and effects vary by storm severity and time horizon. Read the paper

Conclusion

Hurricane Katrina was an insightful natural experiment to understand the effects of natural disasters on labor market outcomes. Our difference-in-differences estimates suggest employment losses in affected counties, but the lack of statistical significance reflects heterogeneity in impact. Results highlight challenges of estimating average effects after large, uneven shocks.

To further test the effect of natural disasters on employment, more natural experiments would be needed: hurricanes in other regions of the world, other types of natural disasters in Louisiana, or other labor market outcomes like establishments.
